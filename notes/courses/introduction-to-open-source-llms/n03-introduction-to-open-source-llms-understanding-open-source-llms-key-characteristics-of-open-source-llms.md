### id8fdXdAe4bA

> [!summary] Cumulative Summary
> Open-source large language models (LLMs) allows the public to view how their algorithm operates to output text, code, or the way it answers questions.

<!-- blockquote separator -->

> [!quote]
> open‑source LLMs provide public access to their model weights. These weights are the result of intensive training and allow a language model to generate text, write code, or answer questions.

<!-- blockquote separator -->

> [!note] Marginalia / Reflection
> A large language models (LLMs) weights are what guides the model to what to output based on its extensive training data.

---

### idr9s4L4H5nR

> [!summary] Cumulative Summary
> Proprietary, closed-source, models "hides" how their model works and interfaces though an Application Programming Interface (API), a black-box, to feed inputs and retrieve feedback from.

<!-- blockquote separator -->

> [!quote]
> With proprietary models such as GPT4 or Gemini, the only way to use the model is through a paid API. There is no access to the underlying technology.

---

### idAb90mgNSxt

> [!summary] Cumulative Summary
> Open-source large language models (LLMs) allows the public, as long as it follows the license policy, review and use their model as they see fit; in contrast, closed-source LLMs obfuscates their algorithm behind an Application Programming Interface (API).
>
> Open models make their algorithms and code open for download, so one is free to, on their own, study, develop on, and distribute without permission of the maintainers (as long as it abides by open license agreement).

<!-- blockquote separator -->

> [!quote]
> open models like Qwen and Mixtral make their weights downloadable for local deployment, meaning you can use, study, and fine‑tune them without gatekeeping.

---

### idgPWz86SlVG

> [!summary] Cumulative Summary
> **Open-source** grants both transparency and usage freedom; in contrast, **closed-source** often comes with a pricing model, and lots of terms-and-conditions.
>
> **Open-source** models allows the public to modify and iterate with experimental or new approaches, and apply and target the model to specific hardware or domain specific data.

<!-- blockquote separator -->

> [!quote]
> Another major trait of open‑source LLMs is the ability to modify the architecture and retrain or fine‑tune the model. Developers can adjust configurations to better suit their hardware, retrain models with industry‑specific data, or experiment with new algorithms and techniques.

<!-- blockquote separator -->

### idaIuW2VC24G

> [!summary] Cumulative Summary
> **Open-source** encourages community development in that open-source does not hide or deny developers from knowing how the algorithm works nor limits developers as how the algorithm can be used (according to the open license agreement).
>
> Open-source Large Language Models (LLMs) is transparent as to what data used and how the model is trained, and how it compares, benchmarked, to other LLMs; as, democratize reproducibility, becomes auditable, and ensures safety standard compliance.

<!-- blockquote separator -->

> [!quote]
> Open‑source LLMs provide public documentation about how models are trained, what data is used, and how they perform on different benchmarks. This enables anyone to reproduce the results, audit for biases, or ensure compliance with safety standards.

---

### id4vIfHImlEe

> [!summary] Cumulative Summary
> Open-source enables the advancement of its own industry, as does in other fields of science, when experiments and observations can be repeated open-source can be iterated on to make conclusions and be made better with each iteration.
>
> Transparency builds trust, so when trust is built communities can build on all previous work of others with trust that the algorithm does not betray or hides key privacy and regulatory requirements.

<!-- blockquote separator -->

> [!quote]
> When the community can follow the entire process from training to deployment, it builds trust and accelerates learning. This level of clarity also allows organizations to meet privacy and regulatory requirements more easily than if they used opaque closed systems.

<!-- blockquote separator -->
